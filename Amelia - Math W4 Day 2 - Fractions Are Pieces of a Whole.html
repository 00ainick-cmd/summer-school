Day 2 · Week 4

Fractions are pieces of a whole

A fraction tells how much of a whole you have. The whole is split into equal parts. You count the parts you took, and you count all the parts. Those two counts make the fraction.

By the end you can write a fraction from a picture and tell what each number stands for.

What this lesson teaches Understand that when a whole is partitioned equally, a fraction can be used to represent a portion of the whole.
01

The two parts of a fraction

A fraction has two numbers. The top counts how many parts you took. The bottom counts how many equal parts the whole has.

Big idea: a fraction is a count over a count. The top tells you about the part. The bottom tells you about the whole.
02

Read the picture, write the fraction

Look at any picture of a whole split into equal parts. To name the fraction, count the shaded parts, count all the parts, and stack them.

2/4
Two shaded, four equal parts in all
2/6
Two shaded, six equal parts in all
3/5
Three shaded, five equal parts in all
3/4
Three shaded, four equal parts in all
Quick tip: count the all of them first. That gives you the bottom number. Then count the shaded ones. That gives you the top.
03

What the parts add up to

When you put all the equal parts back together, you get one whole. So 3/3 is one whole. 5/5 is one whole. 8/8 is one whole.

Big idea: a fraction is a way to talk about part of a whole. Add up all the parts and you are back to one whole.
04

Real wholes have parts

Wholes show up everywhere. A pizza is one whole. A chocolate bar is one whole. A page of stickers is one whole. Each can be split into equal parts and named with a fraction.

5/8 pizza
5 slices eaten out of 8 equal slices
2/3 bar
2 chunks broken off out of 3 equal chunks
4/6 stickers
4 used out of 6 equal stickers
05

Try it: write the fraction

Read each picture. Tap the fraction that matches.

1. A pizza is cut into 8 equal slices. 5 slices are shaded.

Five shaded gives the top: 5. Eight equal parts gives the bottom: 8. The fraction is 5/8.

2. A bar is split into 6 equal parts. 4 parts are shaded.

Four shaded over six equal parts gives 4/6.

3. A circle is cut into 4 equal parts. All 4 parts are shaded.

All four parts shaded is 4/4. When you put every equal part back, you get one whole.
