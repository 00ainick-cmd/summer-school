Reading Historical and Current Maps

A map is a snapshot of a place at one moment. A historical map shows that place a long time ago. A current map shows it today. Put the two beside each other and you can see what changed.

What this lesson teachesRead and construct historical and current maps.
01

First, find the date and source

Every good map tells you when it was made and who made it. That information lives in the corner: a date, a title, and a source. Without those, you cannot tell a historical map from a current one.

Title
What the map is showing.
Date
When the map was made.
Source
Who made it (cartographer or agency).
Legend
What the colors and marks mean.

A snapshot map shows a place at one date. An event map shows what happened, like arrows for a battle or a journey. Both can be historical or current. The date in the corner tells you which.

03

Watch out for this

Common mix-up
An old map is not a wrong map.
The 1800 map is correct for 1800. It does not include California because the United States did not include California yet. Always read the date before you judge a map.

04

Out-loud task

Pretend an adult has never seen these two maps. Walk them through:

Compare and explain

  • Point to the date on each map and read it.
  • Say two things that are the same on both maps.
  • Say two things that changed between 1800 and today.
  • Explain why an 1800 map of the United States is not "wrong" even though it leaves out California.
Adult listener: the goal is that the learner uses the dates to compare, names a real similarity and a real change (border moved west, more states added), and understands that historical maps are snapshots of their own time.
