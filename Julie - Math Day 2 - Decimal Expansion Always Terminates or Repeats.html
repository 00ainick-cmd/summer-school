Session 03

Decimals That Must Behave

Every rational number, divided out, either stops or locks into a repeating block. There is a reason it has to. Today you watch it happen and learn to predict which fractions terminate and which repeat.

What this session teachesUnderstand that all rational numbers have a decimal expansion that terminates or repeats.
01 / LEARN

Three fractions, three behaviors

Look at what long-division gives you for each.

1/4

0.25
Stops. The remainder hits 0 and division ends.

1/3

0.333...
Repeats. The remainder 1 returns each step, so the digit 3 returns.

1/7

0.142857142857...
Repeats with a 6-digit block, because remainders cycle through 1, 3, 2, 6, 4, 5 before returning.
02 / LEARN

Why repetition is forced

When you divide by q, every remainder must be one of 0, 1, 2, ..., q-1. That is only q possibilities. After at most q steps a remainder has to repeat (or be 0). Once a remainder repeats, the next digit repeats, and the next, forever. That is the proof in a sentence.

Engineering shortcut. Reduce p/q first. Then look at the prime factors of q. If the only primes are 2 and 5, the decimal terminates. Anything else in q, and it repeats. So 7/40 terminates (40 = 2³·5), but 7/30 repeats (30 = 2·3·5, has a 3).

05 / EXPLAIN

Write the proof in your own words

In two or three sentences, explain why 1/q always either terminates or repeats. Use the phrase "finitely many remainders."

For your thinking, not graded. Read it back. Did you name the finite-remainder argument?

06 / SAY IT

Out loud, to an adult

Predict whether 7/40 terminates or repeats without dividing, and say why. Then do the same for 5/12.

Adult listener. Look for: (1) factors 40 = 2·2·2·5, only 2s and 5s, so terminates; (2) factors 12 = 2·2·3, the 3 forces repetition.
