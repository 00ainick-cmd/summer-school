Session 01

Citing Evidence in Informational Text

When a writer makes a factual claim about the real world, the burden of proof falls on the cited evidence. Your job as a reader is to locate the specific passage that does the work.

By the end of this session you will distinguish strong evidentiary citations from weaker ones in news articles, expository essays, and source documents, and you will defend why a given quotation most directly substantiates a claim.

What this session teaches Draw conclusions, infer and analyze by citing the textual evidence that most strongly supports an analysis of what the text says explicitly as well as inferences drawn from the text.
01 / LEARN

Expository texts demand specific evidence

An informational text, whether a news article, a science explainer, or a policy brief, advances claims about the actual world. Such claims are defensible only when grounded in cited evidence: a quoted statistic, a named source, a documented event. A vague gesture toward the topic will not suffice. Strong evidentiary citation is specific, verifiable, and directly probative of the claim being defended.

02 / LEARN

Selection criteria for the strongest quotation

CriterionWhat it meansWhy it matters
SpecificityNames a number, a date, a person, or a documented event.Vague language can be marshalled in defense of nearly any claim.
Direct relevanceThe quotation addresses the same idea as the claim, not a neighboring one.An adjacent fact may sound related yet prove nothing about the actual point.
VerifiabilityThe quotation cites a source, a study, or an observable fact.Anonymous assertion is weaker than attributed evidence.

When two quotations both relate to a claim, the stronger one satisfies all three criteria. The weaker one will fail at least one.

03 / LEARN

Explicit statement vs reasoned inference

The standard requires you to cite evidence for two distinct moves. First, for what the text says explicitly: the writer states a fact and your citation is the line that states it. Second, for an inference the text supports but does not state outright. In the second case, the cited evidence must do the heavier lifting; you are arguing that the passage reasonably points toward the conclusion you drew.

MoveExample claimRequired evidence
ExplicitThe author reports that ridership rose 38 percent.The sentence that names the 38 percent figure.
InferredThe author considers the new route a success.Passages that frame the figure positively or contrast it with prior failures.
04 / DO

Select the strongest citation

Read each short passage from a news article or expository source. Then select the quotation that most directly substantiates the stated claim. Some options are factually accurate yet evidentially weak.
