Session 05

Evaluating an Author's Argument

An argument is not just a strong opinion stated loudly. It is a claim, supported by reasoning, supported by evidence. Your job as a reader is to take that structure apart and ask whether the pieces actually hold each other up, or whether something off-topic, fallacious, or emotional has been smuggled in.

What this session teachesEvaluate an author's argument, assessing whether the reasoning is sound and the evidence is relevant and sufficient; recognize when irrelevant evidence is introduced.
01 / LEARN

The structure: claim, reasoning, evidence

Claim

The conclusion the author wants you to accept. Usually one sentence.

Reasoning

The logical bridge: why, if the evidence is true, the claim follows.

Evidence

The facts, data, examples, or sources offered as proof.

An argument is sound when the premises are true, the reasoning is valid, and the evidence is both relevant and sufficient. Take away any one of those and the argument leaks.

02 / LEARN

Four common fallacies to spot

FallacyThe moveExample
Hasty generalizationDrawing a broad claim from too few cases."Two of my classmates failed the test, so the test must be unfair to everyone."
Ad hominemAttacking the person instead of the argument."You cannot trust her data on energy policy. She is barely thirty."
Appeal to popularityTreating popularity as proof."Most people believe it, so it must be correct."
Red herringSwitching to a different issue to dodge the point."We should not raise the speed limit. The real problem is potholes."
03 / LEARN

Relevant vs irrelevant evidence

Even true evidence can be off-topic.

ClaimRelevantIrrelevant
The school should add an after-school engineering club.A survey shows 60 percent of students would attend at least monthly.The school's basketball team won regionals last year.
This brand of running shoe lasts longer.Lab wear-tests show the sole lasts 30 percent more miles.The brand's commercials are funny.

Evidence is relevant when it directly supports the specific claim. It is sufficient when there is enough of it to actually settle the question. Strong feelings are not evidence.
