Session 05

Literary Devices

A literary device is not ornamentation. It is the writer’s instrument for revealing what straight description cannot: the interior of a character, the shape of a setting, the meaning that hovers behind the plot.

By the end of this session you will recognize metaphor, simile, personification, foreshadowing, irony, and symbolism in literary text, and explain how each device develops setting, reveals character, advances plot, or contributes to the work’s meaning.

What this session teaches Analyze how literary devices are used to develop setting, reveal character, advance the plot and contribute to meaning.
01 / LEARN

Figurative comparisons: metaphor, simile, personification

Three closely related devices use figurative language to compare one thing to another so that the reader sees a familiar subject under a new aspect.

DeviceHow it worksExample
MetaphorStates that one thing is another, without "like" or "as.""Hope is a thing with feathers." (Dickinson)
SimileCompares two things using like or as."She stood like a stone in a river of moving people."
PersonificationAttributes human qualities to a nonhuman subject."The wind argued back through the cracks in the window."
02 / LEARN

Structural devices: foreshadowing, irony, motif

Three devices work at the level of structure rather than individual phrasing, shaping the reader’s sense of what is coming, what is hidden, and what is being repeated.

DeviceHow it worksExample
ForeshadowingPlants an early hint of a later development.An opening chapter that mentions a locked drawer the protagonist will eventually open.
IronyReveals a gap between expectation and reality, between what is said and what is meant, or between what a character knows and what the reader knows.A character described as the most reliable witness turns out to be the unreliable narrator.
MotifRepeats an image, phrase, or object across the text to accumulate meaning.A novel that returns to images of mirrors at every turning point.
03 / LEARN

Symbolism: a thing that stands for more than itself

A symbol is an object, image, or action in the text that signifies a larger idea beyond its literal sense. Symbolism becomes legible when the writer returns to the same object at key moments and the object accumulates significance across the text. A single mention is rarely enough; symbolism requires patterning.

Symbol in the textWhat it comes to signify
The green light at the end of the dock (Fitzgerald)The unreachable object of longing.
The conch in Lord of the Flies (Golding)Civilizational order; its breakage signals collapse.
The recurring image of fog in a noir novelMoral obscurity surrounding the investigation.

The standard asks you to do more than identify the device. You must explain what work it does in the text: revealing character, building setting, advancing plot, or contributing to meaning.

04 / DO

Identify the device and its function

Read each short passage. First identify the literary device at work, then identify what the device contributes to setting, character, plot, or meaning.
