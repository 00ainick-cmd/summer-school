Session 16

Diction in Literary Texts

Two writers can describe the same walk down the same hallway and produce opposite stories. The difference is diction. Word choice is not decoration; it is the lever that turns a neutral scene into a heavy one or a buoyant one.

By the end of this session you will identify how specific word choices and sentence structures shape tone and meaning, and you will defend a tone conclusion by pointing to the diction that delivers it.

What this session teaches Analyze how specific word choices and sentence structures contribute to meaning and tone.
01 / LEARN

Diction is the lever

Diction is the writer's specific word choice, considered against the words that could have stood in the same slot. A character can "walk," "trudge," or "skip" down the same hall. The verb does not change where the character is going; it changes how the reader feels about the going. Tone is the attitude the prose holds toward its subject, and diction is its most direct delivery system.

02 / LEARN

Connotation, not just denotation

Two words can name the same action and still carry different freight. The denotation is the dictionary meaning; the connotation is the feeling the word drags with it. A trained reader sorts diction by connotation, then asks what that connotation says about the speaker, the scene, or the work as a whole.

Neutral verbHeavier choiceLighter choice
walktrudge, plod, dragskip, stride, bound
saymutter, hiss, grumblechirp, announce, declare
lookglare, scrutinize, glowerglance, peek, beam
03 / LEARN

Sentence structure carries tone too

Diction is the most visible signal, but sentence structure shapes the same tone reading. A short, clipped sentence lands heavier than a long, flowing one. A fragment can read as urgent or as exhausted, depending on the words around it. Compound sentences with parallel clauses build momentum; a sentence broken into halting pieces stalls the scene.

StructureEffect on tone
Short, clipped sentencesUrgency, exhaustion, or finality.
Long, flowing sentencesDrift, reverie, momentum.
Sentence fragmentsEmphasis, shock, or breathless pace.
Parallel clausesSteady build, rhetorical weight.
