Session 20

Citing Across Multiple Texts

A serious paper draws on more than one source, and the sources are not equal. Some are signed by experts; some are anonymous posts. Your job is to gather smart, judge credibility, and credit every borrowed claim in a clean, standard format.

By the end of this session you will assess source credibility using author, date, and publisher signals, distinguish quoting from paraphrasing, and identify a citation that meets a standard format so a reader can find your source.

What this session teaches Gather relevant information from multiple print and digital sources, using search terms effectively; assess the credibility and accuracy of each source; and quote or paraphrase the data and conclusions of others while avoiding plagiarism and following a standard citation system.
01 / LEARN

Gather smart, search smart

Strong research begins with strong search terms. A broad term like "teen sleep" returns millions of hits; a refined term like "teen sleep school start time study 2022" returns the kind of result a serious paper can use. Mix specific terms, drop common words, and try both general databases and topic-specific ones. Your goal is multiple sources of different types so your paper does not lean on one voice.

02 / LEARN

Credibility lives in three signals

Credibility is the case for trusting a source. The signals to read are author, date, and publisher. A named author with expertise is stronger than an anonymous post. A recent date matters more for fast-moving topics. A reputable publisher, a university press, a peer-reviewed journal, or a government agency, carries more weight than a random blog.

SignalWhat to ask
AuthorIs the author named? Do they have credentials in the field?
DateHow recent is the source? Does the topic move fast?
PublisherUniversity press, peer-reviewed journal, government agency, established outlet?
03 / LEARN

Quote, paraphrase, cite

Quoting copies an author's exact words inside quotation marks. Paraphrasing restates the author's ideas in your own words and sentence patterns. Both must be credited to the source. Using an idea or sentence without credit, even when reworded, is plagiarism. The fix is to follow a standard citation system every time you draw on a source.

MoveWhat it requires
QuoteExact words inside quotation marks; in-text and full citation.
ParaphraseYour words, your sentences; in-text and full citation.
PlagiarismUsing ideas or words without credit; always the wrong move.
04 / LEARN

What a standard citation looks like

A standard citation gives a reader enough information to find the source. Common systems include MLA and APA. The pieces are the same: author, date, title, container, publisher, and a locator like page or URL. Pick one system per paper and stay consistent.

Source typeCitation skeleton (APA style)
BookAuthor, A. (Year). Title. Publisher.
Journal articleAuthor, A. (Year). Title of article. Journal Title, vol(issue), pages.
WebsiteAuthor or Org. (Year). Title of page. Site name. URL.
05 / DO

Decide the credibility move

Each round describes a source you found. Identify what your next move should be: use it, treat it with caution, or replace it.
