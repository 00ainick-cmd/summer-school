Session 09 . Week 2

Body systems interact

No organ system in the human body operates in isolation. Oxygen and nutrients reach a single cell only because the respiratory, digestive, and circulatory systems coordinate. This session traces those pathways and explains why interaction, not specialization alone, is what keeps the organism alive.

What this session teachesPresent evidence that body systems interact to carry out key functions including nutrient and oxygen provision.
01 / FRAME

Why interaction is the point

A multicellular organism gains efficiency by dividing labor: lungs are good at gas exchange, intestines at absorbing nutrients, kidneys at filtering waste. But specialization creates dependency. A muscle cell deep in your thigh cannot breathe and cannot eat. It relies on the respiratory system for oxygen and on the digestive system for glucose, both delivered through the circulatory system. The organism is alive precisely because the systems are coupled.

The key claim. Body systems do not merely coexist; they exchange specific molecules. Tracing those exchanges is how biologists demonstrate system-level interaction.
02 / SYSTEMS

Three systems involved in delivery

Three systems handle the bulk of nutrient and oxygen delivery. Each has a distinct function, and each delivers its product to the next.

Respiratorylungs, trachea, alveoli
Brings air into the body and conducts gas exchange. Oxygen diffuses across the thin alveolar membrane into red blood cells; carbon dioxide diffuses out and is exhaled.
Digestivemouth, stomach, intestines
Breaks food into small molecules through mechanical and chemical digestion. Absorbs glucose, amino acids, and lipids across the small intestine into the bloodstream.
Circulatoryheart, arteries, veins, capillaries
Transports the products of the other two systems to every cell. The heart drives the flow; capillaries are thin enough for oxygen and nutrients to diffuse out and reach individual cells.
03 / TRACE

A single oxygen molecule from air to muscle

Follow one O2 molecule from the moment it enters the nose to the moment it powers ATP production in a working leg muscle. The pathway requires all three systems.

STEP 1The diaphragm contracts. Air containing O2 is drawn through the trachea and bronchi into the alveoli of the lungs.
STEP 2O2 diffuses across the thin alveolar membrane into adjacent capillaries and binds to hemoglobin inside red blood cells.
STEP 3Oxygenated blood returns to the left side of the heart and is pumped out through the aorta.
STEP 4Arteries branch into smaller arterioles and finally into capillary beds inside the leg muscle.
STEP 5O2 diffuses out of the capillary, into the interstitial fluid, and across the plasma membrane of a muscle cell.
STEP 6Inside the muscle cell, O2 enters the mitochondria, where it is consumed in the final stage of cellular respiration to generate ATP.
04 / NUTRIENTS

The nutrient path mirrors the oxygen path

Glucose follows a parallel pathway. Carbohydrates are broken down in the mouth and small intestine by enzymes; the resulting glucose is absorbed across intestinal villi into capillaries. From there, the circulatory system distributes it. The destination is the same cell that needs oxygen, because both molecules are consumed together in cellular respiration. The general equation that the body satisfies at every active cell is:

glucose + 6 O2 ---> 6 CO2 + 6 H2O + ATP
The reactants on the left come from the digestive and respiratory systems. The products on the right are removed by the circulatory system to be exhaled or excreted.
05 / FAILURE

What happens when interaction breaks

The strongest evidence for system interaction is what fails when one part stops. Three scenarios illustrate the dependency.

Pulmonary failure. If the lungs cannot move O2 into the blood, the heart still pumps and the intestines still absorb glucose, but cells cannot complete cellular respiration. ATP production collapses within minutes.
Circulatory arrest. If the heart stops, the lungs may still oxygenate blood and the intestines may still absorb nutrients, but neither product reaches the cells. Cells fail because delivery has failed.
Severe malnutrition. If the digestive system delivers no glucose for an extended period, oxygen still arrives at cells, but the substrate for respiration is missing. Cells switch to alternative fuels and eventually fail.
Inference. The failure modes confirm that no system is redundant. Each is necessary; together they are sufficient.
06 / APPLY

Predict the limiting system

An athlete sprints to the point of muscle burning and dizziness. Blood glucose is normal. Hemoglobin saturation in arterial blood is normal. Heart rate is at maximum. The most likely immediate limiting factor for sustained sprinting is ...

When intake and absorption are both normal, the bottleneck is throughput. The circulatory system has to deliver oxygen and clear CO2 at the rate the muscle is consuming and producing them. Maximum heart rate sets the ceiling on that rate. The burning sensation indicates that lactic acid is accumulating because the circulatory delivery has fallen behind muscle demand.
