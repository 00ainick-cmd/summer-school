Fossil record and extinction

Layered sedimentary rock is a chronological archive. Older strata sit below younger strata. The fossils within each layer mark which organisms lived during that interval, and the appearance and disappearance of species across layers document environmental change and extinction events. This session walks through how to read those layers as data.

What this session teachesAnalyze and interpret evidence from the fossil record to infer patterns of environmental change and extinction.
01

Why fossil layers are an archive

Sedimentary rock forms when particles settle and compress over long timescales. Younger sediment is deposited on top of older sediment. The principle of superposition states that, in undisturbed strata, lower layers are older than upper layers. Fossils preserved within a layer record which organisms lived during the interval when that layer was being deposited.

Index fossils are particularly useful: species that were widespread and existed only for a short period are reliable markers for the age of a layer. A trilobite is an index fossil for the Paleozoic; if a layer contains trilobites but no dinosaur bones, it predates the Mesozoic.

Reading the column. A vertical cross-section through sedimentary rock is read from bottom (oldest) to top (youngest). What appears, what disappears, and what is replaced gives you the pattern.
02

Patterns in the record

Three distinct patterns are routinely inferred from fossil sequences. Each is an interpretation supported by what the rock record contains and what it lacks.

Environmental change
A layer that contains marine fossils (coral, shells) sitting beneath a layer that contains terrestrial fossils (trees, land mammals) indicates that the environment shifted from sea to land. The fossils document what lived there at each point in time.
Extinction
When a species appears consistently in lower layers, then disappears completely from all higher layers, the most parsimonious interpretation is extinction. The absence of the species from every younger layer is the evidence.
Mass extinction event
When many unrelated species disappear from the same horizontal boundary, a major event affected the biosphere globally. The K-Pg boundary (about 66 million years ago) marks the end of non-avian dinosaurs and many other groups; the Permian-Triassic boundary (about 252 million years ago) marks the largest known mass extinction.
03

Inference vs assertion

The fossil record supports inferences, not direct observation. A scientist does not see dinosaurs die. The scientist sees a pattern: dinosaur fossils present below a boundary, absent above it. From that pattern, the scientist infers extinction at that boundary.

Strong inference

  • Multiple sites show the same pattern.
  • The boundary is dated consistently with independent methods.
  • An alternate explanation (e.g., migration) is ruled out by global absence.
  • The pattern fits other geological evidence (e.g., iridium layer).

Weak inference

  • One site, small sample.
  • Disturbed strata of uncertain order.
  • Species could simply be undiscovered in higher layers.
  • No corroborating evidence beyond fossils.
Sampling caveat. Absence of a fossil in a layer can mean extinction, but it can also mean the layer was a poor place for fossilization or the species was rare and never preserved there. Strong claims require strong sampling.
04

Read a sequence

Consider the column above. From the bottom up: a Precambrian layer with only microbial mats; a Paleozoic layer rich in trilobites and other marine invertebrates; a sharp boundary representing the Permian-Triassic event; a Mesozoic layer with dinosaur fossils; another sharp boundary (K-Pg); a Cenozoic layer with mammals.

From this column you can infer: (1) life became more complex over time, (2) trilobites went extinct at the Permian-Triassic boundary, (3) dinosaurs went extinct at the K-Pg boundary, (4) the environment in this location was marine during the Paleozoic and shifted to terrestrial later, and (5) major mass extinctions reset the dominant fauna more than once. Each inference is supported by what is present in some layers and absent in others.

05

Interpret one

A paleontologist finds trilobite fossils in a layer dated at 400 million years ago. A layer directly above, dated at 240 million years ago, contains no trilobites at all, despite extensive sampling. Higher layers also contain no trilobites. Which interpretation is best supported?

Trilobites are present below a boundary and absent in all later layers worldwide. The strongest interpretation is global extinction. Migration cannot account for total absence across continents, and modern surveys confirm no living trilobites.
