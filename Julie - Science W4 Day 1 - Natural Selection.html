Natural selection

The fossil record shows that species change over geologic time. Natural selection is the mechanism that drives much of that change. Trait variation already exists in every population; the environment then filters which variants survive and reproduce. Over many generations, the frequency of advantageous traits rises and the frequency of disadvantageous traits falls. This session walks through the logic, then quantifies it on a sample population.

What this session teachesUse mathematical representations to support explanations of how natural selection may lead to increases and decreases of specific traits in populations over time.
01

The four conditions

Natural selection operates whenever four conditions hold within a population. Strip away any one, and the mechanism stalls. The four conditions are observable in any species that reproduces sexually, and most that do not.

Variation
Individuals in a population differ in their traits. Some moths are lighter, some darker; some finches have longer beaks, some shorter. Without variation, there is nothing for selection to act on.
Heritability
The variation is at least partly heritable. Parents pass on the genetic basis of their traits to offspring, so trait differences carry across generations rather than washing out each cycle.
Differential survival or reproduction
Some variants are more likely to survive long enough to reproduce, or to produce more offspring per reproductive attempt. The environment is the filter; predators, weather, food supply, and disease all contribute.
Time across generations
The shift in trait frequency is generational, not within a single lifetime. An organism does not evolve. A population does, as the makeup of each new generation reflects which traits made it to reproduction.
Watch the language. Individuals do not adapt. Populations shift. A single moth does not turn darker because predators are eating its lighter cousins; rather, the darker moths leave more offspring, so the next generation contains a higher fraction of dark moths.
02

Quantify the shift

Mathematical representations make the abstract logic concrete. Consider a population of 100 moths. Begin with 60 light-colored and 40 dark-colored. Predators on bark darkened by industrial soot eat moths they can see; light moths stand out, dark moths blend in. Suppose 40 percent of light moths and 10 percent of dark moths are eaten before reproducing, and the survivors reproduce equally.

Notice what is happening. The starting fractions for each new generation reflect the survivor fractions from the previous one. Dark frequency climbs from 40 percent to 50 percent to 60 percent to 69 percent across just three generations of predation. The trait did not appear from nowhere; it was already present in Gen 1. Selection only changed how common it became.

03

What selection is not

Common misconceptions distort the concept badly. Three deserve direct refutation.

Misconception

  • Organisms develop the traits they need.
  • Mutations occur because they are useful.
  • Evolution has a goal or direction toward complexity.
  • Stronger always wins; selection is survival of the toughest.

What actually happens

  • Variation arises through random mutation and sexual recombination, independent of need.
  • Mutation is undirected. Useful and useless mutations occur at similar rates; the environment then sorts them.
  • Selection is local and current. A trait useful in this environment may be useless in another. There is no aim.
  • Reproductive success, not raw strength, is what counts. A meek but well-camouflaged moth out-reproduces a strong but visible one.
The trap. Selection acts on phenotypes, but only changes in heritable genotypes persist across generations. A weightlifter who builds muscle does not pass that muscle on; the children inherit whatever genetic potential the parent had, not the parent's lived adaptations.
04

Mathematical logic, step by step

Translate the four conditions into a simple repeating calculation. Define p as the frequency of the favored trait in this generation. The new frequency p' after one round of selection is:

p' = (p . s_favored) / [(p . s_favored) + ((1 - p) . s_other)]

Here s_favored is the survival rate of the favored variant and s_other is the survival rate of the alternative. In our moth example, p (dark frequency) starts at 0.40, s_favored is 0.90, and s_other is 0.60. Plug in: p' = (0.40 . 0.90) / [(0.40 . 0.90) + (0.60 . 0.60)] = 0.36 / (0.36 + 0.36) = 0.50. That matches Gen 1 survivors at 50 percent dark. Iterate the same formula and the frequency keeps climbing as long as the survival difference persists.

The formula in words. The new frequency is the share of the favored variant in the survivor pool. If the favored variant survives more often, its share of the survivor pool exceeds its share before selection, and the next generation begins with a higher starting frequency.
05

Apply it

A population of 200 beetles lives on green leaves. Beetles come in two colors: 120 are brown and 80 are green. A bird predator eats 50 percent of brown beetles and only 15 percent of green beetles each generation. Which of the following is the best prediction for the population after several generations?

Heavier predation on brown beetles means brown survivors contribute fewer offspring to the next generation. Green frequency rises with every cycle until predation pressure shifts. Individual beetles do not change color. The population composition shifts because the survivor pool is enriched in green.
