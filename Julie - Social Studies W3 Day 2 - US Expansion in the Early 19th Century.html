Week 3 . Day 2 . Expansion

US expansion in the early 19th century

In 1800, the United States stopped at the Mississippi River. Within fifty years, it stretched to the Pacific. Each new acquisition pushed the borders west, doubled or tripled the country in stages, and absorbed land that other nations and Indigenous peoples already claimed. Tracing that growth predicts the conflicts that followed.

By the end, you can sequence the major acquisitions between 1803 and 1848, identify how each one was obtained, and form hypotheses about the conflicts each set in motion.

Verbatim Missouri standard Analyze the expansion of the United States in the early nineteenth century to trace U.S. growth and form hypotheses about future conflicts.
01

Starting borders, 1800

At the start of the nineteenth century, the United States covered about 864,000 square miles between the Atlantic and the Mississippi River. France held the vast Louisiana Territory to the west. Spain held Florida, Texas, and the southwest. Britain held Canada to the north. Indigenous nations controlled most of the interior. Any move west would touch a European empire, an Indigenous polity, or both.

02

Five stages of growth

In a single generation, the country bought, negotiated, annexed, and conquered its way to the Pacific. Each step doubled the stakes for the next one.

1803

Louisiana Purchase

Jefferson bought 828,000 square miles from Napoleon for fifteen million dollars. The country doubled in size overnight. Most of present-day Missouri, Iowa, Arkansas, Kansas, Nebraska, and the Dakotas came in with one signature. The new land had to be surveyed, governed, and shared with the Indigenous nations already living there.

1819

Adams-Onis Treaty

Spain ceded Florida and clarified the western border of the Louisiana Purchase. The treaty drew a line from the Gulf of Mexico to the Pacific that fixed the Spanish-American frontier. Spain was weakening; the United States moved into the opening.

1845

Texas annexation

American settlers had moved into Mexican Texas in the 1820s and declared independence in 1836. After nine years as a separate republic, Texas joined the United States. Mexico considered the act an act of war.

1846

Oregon Treaty

The United States and Britain split the Oregon Country at the 49th parallel. American claims now reached the Pacific without bloodshed.

1848

Mexican Cession

The U.S. Mexican War ended with the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo. Mexico gave up roughly 525,000 square miles, what would become California, Nevada, Utah, most of Arizona and New Mexico, and parts of Colorado and Wyoming. The transcontinental United States was complete.

03

How each acquisition happened

The means varied. Two were purchases. One was a treaty. One was an annexation. One was a war.

Purchase

Louisiana, 1803. A direct cash purchase from France. No fighting on U.S. soil; the negotiation was diplomatic.

Treaty

Florida 1819, Oregon 1846. Treaties resolved competing claims with European powers in decline or unwilling to fight a war over distant land.

Annexation

Texas, 1845. An independent republic chose to join. A joint resolution of Congress completed the act. Mexico, which had never recognized Texas independence, rejected the result.

Conquest

Mexican Cession, 1848. Won on the battlefield in the U.S. Mexican War. The cession was the territorial price Mexico paid in defeat.

04

Predicting future conflicts

Each acquisition planted the seeds of a later conflict. Hypothesis formation begins by asking what tensions the new map created.

Slavery in new territory

Every new territory raised the question of whether slavery would be permitted there. The Missouri Compromise of 1820, the Compromise of 1850, and Bleeding Kansas in the 1850s all traced back to land brought in between 1803 and 1848. Hypothesis: territorial growth before 1850 made the Civil War of 1861 to 1865 more likely.

Indigenous displacement

The land was already inhabited. Federal policy treated expansion as compatible with removing Indigenous nations from the path of white settlement. Hypothesis: rapid expansion accelerated the forced removals of the 1830s, the Plains Wars, and the long destruction of Indigenous land tenure.

Mexican-American relations

Texas annexation and the Mexican Cession transferred land Mexico considered its own. Hypothesis: the 1848 boundary set up a century of border disputes, migration questions, and grievances that still shape U.S. Mexico relations.

05

Sequence check

Place the acquisitions in order from earliest to latest. Which option lists them correctly?

Louisiana 1803, Florida 1819, Texas 1845, Mexican Cession 1848. The order matters because each step set up the next.
