Week 4 . Day 5 . Civil War

The Civil War: crisis to conclusion

The American Civil War lasted four years, from April 1861 to April 1865. Roughly 750,000 soldiers died, more Americans than in every other U.S. war combined until Vietnam. The Union won; the Confederacy collapsed; slavery was abolished by the Thirteenth Amendment in December 1865. The war was the largest constitutional crisis in U.S. history and the most consequential. This session walks through the major events, turning points, and outcomes that defined it.

By the end, you can name the major battles and decisions that shaped the war, place them in order, and explain why the Union won and what the war's outcomes meant for the nation.

Verbatim Missouri standard Analyze the political, social, economic, cultural, and military aspects of the Civil War and its consequences.
01

The two sides at the start

When the war began in April 1861, the Union and the Confederacy held very different strategic positions. The disparity in resources became increasingly decisive as the war stretched on.

Union (23 states)

  • About 22 million people; large industrial base concentrated in the Northeast.
  • Roughly 90 percent of U.S. manufacturing, almost all firearms production, and most railroad mileage.
  • Powerful navy; ability to blockade Confederate ports.
  • Federal government with established institutions and access to international credit.
  • Goal: preserve the Union and (after January 1863) abolish slavery.

Confederacy (11 states)

  • About 9 million people, including 3.5 million enslaved Black Americans whom Confederate law refused to enlist or arm.
  • Plantation agriculture; limited industry; cotton dependent on export markets blocked by Union blockade.
  • Strong officer tradition; many West Point graduates joined Confederate forces.
  • Fighting a defensive war on familiar terrain.
  • Goal: independence and the perpetuation of slavery.

The Anaconda strategy

The Union plan, sketched by General Winfield Scott, called for a naval blockade of the southern coast and a thrust down the Mississippi River to split the Confederacy in two. The strategy was slow and unglamorous, and it took years to execute, but it steadily strangled Confederate trade, supplies, and mobility.

02

Six events, in order

The military and political history of the war can be tracked through six pivotal events that together shifted both the battlefield and the political meaning of the conflict.

Apr 1861

Fort Sumter

Confederate forces fired on the federal garrison at Fort Sumter in Charleston harbor on April 12 and forced its surrender. President Lincoln called for 75,000 volunteers; four additional states seceded in response. The war was on.

Sep 1862

Antietam

Confederate General Robert E. Lee invaded Maryland to bring the war to the North and seek European recognition. Union General George McClellan stopped him at Antietam Creek on September 17, the single bloodiest day in U.S. history with roughly 23,000 casualties combined. Lee withdrew. The tactical draw was a strategic Union victory.

Jan 1863

Emancipation Proclamation

Using his war powers, Lincoln declared all enslaved persons in Confederate-held territory free, effective January 1, 1863. The Proclamation transformed the war aim from preserving the Union to ending slavery. It enabled Black soldiers to enlist in the Union Army; nearly 200,000 served by the war's end.

Jul 1863

Gettysburg and Vicksburg

Two Union victories arrived within a day of each other. At Gettysburg, Pennsylvania, Lee's second invasion of the North was halted in a three-day battle (July 1 to 3). The Confederate Army never again threatened the North on the same scale. At Vicksburg, Mississippi, General Ulysses S. Grant's forces captured the city on July 4, completing Union control of the Mississippi River and splitting the Confederacy.

Nov 1864

Sherman's March

Union General William T. Sherman captured Atlanta in September 1864, then marched his army across Georgia to Savannah, destroying railroads, factories, and farms to break the South's capacity and will to continue. The campaign demonstrated the Union's total-war doctrine and helped secure Lincoln's reelection that November.

Apr 1865

Appomattox Court House

After Petersburg fell and Richmond was evacuated, Lee's army was cornered in Virginia. On April 9, 1865, Lee surrendered to Grant at Appomattox Court House. Within weeks, remaining Confederate forces surrendered. The war was over. Five days after Appomattox, President Lincoln was assassinated by John Wilkes Booth.

03

Why the Union won

Historians identify several converging factors. No single factor explains the outcome alone; the combination is what proved decisive.

Industrial and demographic advantage

The North's population was roughly 2.5 times larger than the white population of the South, and its industrial output was an order of magnitude greater. The Union could replace soldiers, equipment, and rolling stock at rates the Confederacy could not match. By 1864 the Union army was larger than the Confederate army had ever been.

Naval blockade and Mississippi control

The Union navy blockaded southern ports throughout the war, choking exports of cotton and imports of weapons. Capture of New Orleans in 1862 and Vicksburg in 1863 gave the Union control of the Mississippi River, splitting Confederate territory in half and severing Texas and Arkansas from the eastern Confederacy.

Shifted war aims and Black soldiers

The Emancipation Proclamation gave the Union a moral cause that mobilized northern public opinion and made European recognition of the Confederacy politically impossible. Nearly 200,000 Black soldiers and sailors served the Union by war's end, adding manpower the Confederacy refused to use on its own side.

Generalship and total war by 1864

Lincoln found in Grant a commander who would press the Confederate armies relentlessly. Sherman's campaign across Georgia and Sheridan's in the Shenandoah Valley applied a total-war doctrine that targeted Confederate capacity, not just Confederate armies.

04

What the war settled

Three major outcomes of the war reshaped the country, though many issues the war raised were left for the decades of Reconstruction and beyond.

Union preserved
The constitutional question of whether states could secede was settled by force. No state has tried to leave the Union since. The federal government emerged from the war substantially more powerful relative to the states than it had been before.
Slavery abolished
The Thirteenth Amendment, ratified December 1865, ended slavery throughout the United States. Roughly 4 million people were freed. The Fourteenth Amendment (1868) established birthright citizenship and equal protection; the Fifteenth (1870) guaranteed voting rights regardless of race, though enforcement remained contested for a century.
Human cost
Roughly 750,000 soldiers died, plus an unknown number of civilians. Large parts of the South were physically destroyed. The political and economic settlements of the next twelve years (Reconstruction) determined the meaning of Union victory in practice; many of those settlements were rolled back by 1877.
05

Read a sequence

A student examines the events of 1862 and 1863 and observes the following: Union forces stop Lee at Antietam in September 1862; Lincoln issues the Emancipation Proclamation effective January 1863; Lee invades the North again and is defeated at Gettysburg in July 1863; on the next day, Vicksburg falls, giving the Union the Mississippi. Which interpretation is best supported?

Antietam enabled the Proclamation. The Proclamation reframed the war as a war against slavery. Gettysburg ended Lee's capacity to invade the North. Vicksburg gave the Union the Mississippi. Together, these are the strategic and political pivot of the war. The Confederacy never recovered.
