Greek and Latin Academic Roots

Roughly sixty percent of English vocabulary derives from Latin and Greek. The roots that built scholarly English are the building blocks behind most tier-three academic vocabulary: hydrology, chronology, demography, hypothesis, photosynthesis. Once you recognize the root, the spelling becomes structural rather than arbitrary. This session presents ten high-frequency academic words built from common roots, names the root in each, and drills both spelling and the underlying meaning.

What this session teachesdemonstrate a command of the conventions of standard English grammar and usage, including spelling and punctuation.

Tactics
  • Identify the root first. A long word is a sequence of small parts; name each one and the spelling becomes structural.
  • Watch for Greek "y" vs Latin "i". Greek-origin words often carry a Y where you might expect an I (hydrology not hidrology; hypothesis not hipothesis). Latin-origin words generally use I.
  • Watch the connecting vowel. Greek compounds typically link with O (chrono-logy, photo-synthesis). Latin compounds vary, but I and U are more common (struct-ure).
  • Suffixes follow patterns. -logy means study of. -graph means writing or recording. -meter means measure. -sis often signals a process or condition.
  • If you can spell the root once, you can usually spell it everywhere it appears.

04 / WATCH

Common spelling traps

Common error
"I will sound it out and that will tell me the spelling."
For root-based academic vocabulary, sound is the trap. Hypothesis sounds like "hi-poth-eh-sis," which suggests hipothesis; the H is silent and the Y looks wrong. Chronology sounds like "kron-ol-uh-jee," which suggests kronology; the CH is borrowed from Greek and writes the K sound. Photograph sounds like "fo-toh-graf," which suggests fotograph. The fix is to memorize the root spelling once: HYPO, CHRONO, PHOTO. Then every word built from those roots inherits the spelling.
05 / RECAP

The move

Remember:
  • Decompose every long academic word into its roots before you write it.
  • Spell the root once correctly and the variants follow predictably.
  • Greek roots favor Y, PH, and CH; Latin roots tend to use I, F, and K. Use that pattern as a check.
  • Connecting vowels (typically O for Greek) join roots together cleanly. Notice them when you write.
