Chapter five

Tricky Nouns

Most of the time, more than one means add an s. One cup, two cups. One dog, two dogs. Easy. But some words do not play that game. One child, two children. One foot, two feet. These are tricky nouns. You learn them by heart.

By the end you will know the tricky noun pairs. You will pick the right word when you mean one, and the right word when you mean more than one.

What this lesson teachesUse common irregular nouns.
01

How most words work

For most words, you just add s to mean more than one. This is the normal way.

normal pairs
one cup → two cups. one dog → two dogs. one toy → two toys.

Add an s. Done. That is the rule for most words.

02

03

See them in sentences

Match the word to the count. One thing = the short word. Two or more = the long form.

One child went down the slide. Then five children went after.
A mouse sat by the cheese. Soon three mice were there.
My left foot hurts. Both feet are tired.
The woman waved. Then more women joined her.
04

How to remember them

Tricky words show up a lot in books. Read them. Say them. They start to stick.

Try this rhyme in your head: one foot, two feet. one tooth, two teeth. The oo turns to ee. That helps you spot a few of them.

For the rest, just hold the pair in your mind: child / children, mouse / mice, man / men, woman / women, goose / geese.

05

Pick the right form

Read each sentence. Tap the word that fits.

06

Watch out for this

Common mix-up
Adding s to a tricky word.
Words like foots, mouses, childs, tooths: those are not real words. The plural is the whole new word. Feet, mice, children, teeth. No s.
07

The move

Remember:
  • Most words just add an s for more than one. Cups. Dogs.
  • Tricky words turn into a new word. Children, mice, feet, women, men, teeth, geese.
  • Never add s to a tricky word. No foots. No mouses.
  • Count first. One = the short form. More than one = the new form.
